History: The Archives

Origins

In the present day, the commonplace book is seen as a personal notebook. However, the word “commonplace” traces back to Ancient Greece. Speakers in law courts and political meetings kept an assortment of arguments in a “common place” to quickly refer to. The Romans later called them locus communis, meaning a “theme or arguments of general application”. Over time, the methods began to evolve and become a way for readers to preserve observations, knowledge, and excerpts that they found worth keeping.

Renaissance Readers

There was a span of time where commonplace books weren't commonly used, but during the Renaissance, they became a popular method for readers to collect quotes and insights from texts for their personal use.

Famous Commonplacers

John Locke

John Locke (1632-1704) was an influential English philosopher, and physician during the Enlightenment movement. Known for his foundational work in epistemology and political philosophy. He is also remembered for his approach to commonplace books. In 1685, he developed an indexing method in his essay titled A New Method of Making Common-Place Books. Locke's commonplace book was an organized, indexed repository that was designed to keep records of excerpts, reflections, and citations without forcing entries into rigid categories or from having the dilemma of a disorganized notebook.

Thomas Jefferson

In the 18th Century, the 3rd President of the United States Thomas Jefferson kept both literature and legal based commonplace books. He would include topics of interest such as politics, history, law, and philosophy.
